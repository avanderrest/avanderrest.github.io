How it works

Each picture is drawn by a small machine following the rules of an artist who worked by rules. Nothing is chosen by hand: every “random” decision comes from the seed, so a seed always gives back exactly the same picture. Roll the 🎲 for a new one, or type any word.

Colours. Drop in a photo and the machine finds its five main colours and how much of the picture each one covers. Those shares carry through: a colour that fills 40% of your photo gets 40% of a chance grid. The most common colour becomes the ground. With no photo, the seed mixes a palette of its own.

Watch it draw builds the picture again piece by piece, the way a plotter would. Set it moving brings it to life: panels swap places, squares turn, grids drift, waves roll, strands flow along their currents like water. Hold still puts the seed’s picture back.

  • Chance grid — after Ellsworth Kelly’s Spectrum Colors Arranged by Chance and Sol LeWitt’s wall drawings: a grid dealt out in exact colour shares, as flat panels or LeWitt’s four kinds of line.
  • Disrupted grid — after Vera Molnár’s (Dés)ordres: nested squares on a strict grid, nudged by a growing “machine error”, with a few left out.
  • Gravel — after Georg Nees’s Schotter and Frieder Nake: neat rows of squares or strokes that fall into Gaussian disorder further down, or further out.
  • Superimposed — after François Morellet: grids of parallel lines laid over one another. Choose π and the angles come from the digits of pi.
  • Bulging grid — after Victor Vasarely’s Vega: a chequerboard of shapes pushed out through a lens until the flat grid swells. With a photo, the bulge can sit on its brightest part.
  • Waves — after Bridget Riley’s Cataract paintings: stripes bent by a sine wave. With a photo, its bright parts make the waves swell.
  • Flow field — after Tyler Hobbs: ribbons that follow a smooth field of directions and stop before they touch.
  • Process — after Casey Reas: unseen circles drift about, and wherever two overlap a faint line joins their centres. The picture is what those lines leave behind.
  • Mosaic — after Ken Knowlton’s computer mosaics at Bell Labs: your photo rebuilt from small symbols or letters, each picked for how much ink it puts down. Best with a photo.

Copy link keeps the seed, the style, the settings and the photo’s colours, so whoever opens it sees exactly this picture, without needing the photo.
